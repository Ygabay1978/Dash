Passport

Passport is a central governance layer for discovering, requesting access to, and securely consuming APIs across the organization — without exposing Secrets.

⠿

Think of it this way: the more APIs there are, and the more people, applications and AI Agents that need to use them, the harder it gets to know who's using what, who has access, and where the actual API keys and Secrets live. A developer looks for an API on Slack or in the docs, asks someone for access, and the real key ends up in .env files, scripts, and CI/CD in a dozen places. Passport gives a central governance layer for discovering, requesting access to, and securely consuming APIs — without ever exposing the actual Secrets.

Central Discovery
Developers and AI Agents find approved APIs in one place
Controlled Access Request
Instead of getting the real key, they get a Passport Reference
Secretless Consumption
The real Credential stays in the customer's existing Vault, resolved only at the moment of the API call
Full Visibility
Who requested access, who approved it, and how the API is actually used

In one sentence: Passport gives the organization a central way to discover APIs, manage access to them, and consume them securely — without ever exposing the real Credentials.

From here, move to the problem

A new team is building an app that needs an internal customer API. The developer has to find out who owns the API, who to ask, get a key, and wire it into the app. Multiply that by dozens of APIs and hundreds of developers — and at some point the organization simply doesn't know who has which key or access to which API. And as more AI Agents enter the picture asking for API access, this challenge only grows.

Discovery questions — including the two key questions
⠿
First scenario: an insurance company
An insurance company with external agents and AI Agents

External insurance agents, partners, and internal AI Agents all need access to policy and claims APIs. Today, access is managed by hand — a ticket, an approval, a key emailed or sent on Slack.

The problem: when an agent or partner stops working with the company, there's no guarantee anyone remembers to revoke their access. Passport gives an orderly access request, approval, and limited validity — and lets you revoke access from one place the moment you need to.

Who to target: Head of API/Platform (the Lead), CISO/Compliance (audit trail).

Discovery question to close the scenario"If an outside agent stops working with you today, how long does it take to know for sure their API access has been revoked?"
⠿
Second scenario: a credit card company
A credit card company with Fraud/Risk teams accessing Payment APIs

Fraud, Risk and outside partner teams all need access to sensitive Payment APIs. There's a real concern (and usually a PCI requirement) that API keys will leak into Repos, Logs, or local files.

Passport reduces the need to distribute the real key at all — instead, a Reference travels, and the Credential itself stays in the Vault, resolved only at the moment of the call. That's exactly the anchor the Compliance team is looking for.

Who to target: Head of API Platform (the Lead), Risk/Compliance (a quiet but important sponsor).

⠿
Third scenario: a bank
A bank with dozens of dev teams

At a bank with, say, 40 dev teams, you need to stop the payments team from touching the HR workspace — without anyone manually managing that every day. And as more internal AI Agents enter the picture, "who can even reach what" becomes a critical question.

Passport gives the organization one governance layer: central discovery, an orderly access request, and secure consumption without exposing Secrets — with a full audit trail a regulator can check.

Who to target: Head of API Architecture / Platform (the Lead), Compliance/Risk (a critical partner).

Discovery Questions

  • If a key leaked into a Repo or a Log today, how long would it take to find out?
    If the answer isn't clear, that's the pain.
  • Who approves access to a new API today, and how is that documented?
    If the answer is "Slack" or "whoever's free" — that's a strong signal.
  • How does a developer discover and request access to an internal API today?
  • Do you have a way to know who currently holds which keys?
  • Do you use HashiCorp / AWS Secrets Manager / 1Password?
  • AI Agents need access to your APIs — how is that managed today?

Common objections

"We already have a Vault (HashiCorp/AWS/1Password)"
AEGreat — Passport doesn't replace the Vault, it adds an Access Governance layer on top: who requests, who approves, and how it's consumed.
"This is another approval layer that will slow us down"
AEThe key itself doesn't change at runtime — what changes is transparency: who requested, who approved, what got consumed. It adds no latency to the actual API call.
"We trust our developers"
AEIt's not about trust — it's about what happens when someone leaves, when a key accidentally hits a public Repo, or when a regulator asks exactly who accessed what over the last year.
